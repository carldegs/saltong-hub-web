As we get closer to the 2000th Saltong round, we’re starting to find patterns and little tricks that can help solve the puzzle faster and with fewer guesses.

Personally, I started having my own set of starter words: **ITLOG** for the classic game, **PALABOK** for Saltong Max, and **BATI** for Saltong Mini.

Were they the statistically best words to use? Probably not. They worked for me, though, and after a while they just became part of the routine.

But I wanted to get better at it, so I scoured the internet for tips on the OG game, Wordle. And there are a lot of them: start with as many vowels as possible, use common consonants, avoid repeated letters early, pick opening words like CRANE or ADIEU.

A lot of those tips are useful in Saltong too. Saltong did, after all, start as the Filipino Wordle.

But not everything translates perfectly.

Filipino is a different ballgame altogether, with different letter frequencies, syllable patterns, affixes, loanwords from different cultures, and a few quirks that don’t really come up when you’re playing an English word game. Strategies designed around English words won’t always work the same way in a Tagalog Wordle or any other Filipino word game.

So after reading way too many Wordle tips and, somehow, revisiting a few Filipino lessons I probably should have remembered from elementary school, here are some things that might help improve your **Saltong** game.

## 1. Get to know your PaPa

Okay, not that *papa*.

I’m talking about your **pantig** and **panlapi**.

For anyone who, like me, has forgotten a good chunk of Grade 4 Filipino:

> P.S. I literally had to relearn some of this from a [Grade 4 Filipino lesson](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/comprehensive-filipino-language-lessons-for-grade-4-panlapi-pangatnig-pangngalan-panghalip-and-pang-uri/288396574). Thank you, Teacher Suzette.

**Pantig** is a syllable, or one beat of pronunciation in a word. These are your **MA**, **PA**, **KI**, **DO**, and so on.

**Panlapi** are affixes added to a root word, or *salitang ugat*, to change its meaning or grammatical function.

For example:

- **Unlapi** or prefix: **mag**-aral
- **Gitlapi** or infix: s**um**ayaw
- **Hulapi** or suffix: basa**han**
- Or a combination: **pag** + **sum** + *sikap* + **an** = **pagsumikapan**

These patterns are a big part of how Filipino words are built. So when you’re solving Saltong, don’t always look at the board as five or seven completely separate letters.

Look for chunks.

If you see something that resembles **MA-**, **PA-**, **-UM-**, **-IN-**, or **-AN**, consider whether you’re looking at a root word plus an affix.

The same goes for *pantig*.

Filipino syllables are generally built around vowels, and more complex consonant clusters are especially common in borrowed words. So if you see an unusual cluster, that can itself be a clue that you might be dealing with a loanword.

| Pattern | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| P | **a**-**a**-sa |
| KP | gi-ta-ra |
| PKK | eks-tra |
| KKP | tse-ke, dra-ku-la |
| KKPKK | tsart |

A more in-depth discussion of Filipino syllable structures can be found [here](https://www.scribd.com/presentation/730177353/Week-5-Ortograpiya-ng-Wikang-Filipino).

The practical takeaway is simple: if you’re stuck, stop staring at the letters one by one. Try to hear the word. Break it into *pantig* and look for familiar *panlapi*.

## 2. Tagalog loves its As

The modern Filipino alphabet has **28 letters**, while the older *Abakada* had 20 and even treated **Ng** as a separate letter.

The alphabet expanded partly to accommodate words from other Philippine languages and borrowed vocabulary.

But even the letters we share with English don’t show up at the same rates.

**A** is a particularly good example.

One letter-frequency analysis used a corpus of **368,905 Filipino words** and more than **1.7 million counted characters**. In that corpus, **A accounted for 24.25% of all letters**, followed by **N at 11.77%** and **G at 8.51%**.

That is a lot of As.

So when in doubt, testing **A** is usually not a bad idea.

And once you find it, you can start thinking about familiar combinations like **KA**, **MA**, **NA**, **PA**, **SA**, and **-AN**.

The same idea works in the other direction. If you uncover something like **F**, **J**, **V**, **Z**, or **C**, don’t immediately assume you’re off track. Filipino contains plenty of words borrowed from Spanish, English, and other languages.

Sometimes an unusual-looking letter is actually telling you what kind of word to look for.

## 3. Keep common consonant combinations in mind

Speaking of *Abakada*, **Ng** was historically treated as a distinct letter, and it remains one of the most recognizable sound combinations in Filipino.

So if you’ve already found both **N** and **G**, it’s worth checking whether they belong together.

There are other consonant combinations worth keeping in mind too:

- **MB** and **MP** — *samba*, *lampa*, *tampo*
- **NT**, **ND**, and **NS** — *banta*, *banda*, *bansa*

These aren’t hard rules, and not every N and G will become **NG**. But once you start recognizing common Filipino sound patterns, you stop treating the board as a random collection of letters and start narrowing down words much faster.

## 4. We really like to repeat stuff

Reduplication, or repeating all or part of a word, is a major feature of Filipino and many other Austronesian languages.

I think we all know **Bababa ba? Bababa.** by now.

In Filipino, repeated sounds and syllables can be part of how words are formed. That makes this especially useful to remember in Saltong Max, where longer words give those patterns more room to appear.

Think of words like **susulat** and **tatakbo**.

The useful Saltong lesson here is not that every repeated letter means reduplication. It’s simply this:

**Don’t assume every blank needs a new letter.**

A common Wordle tip is to avoid repeated letters in your first guess because five unique letters give you more information. That still makes sense. But later in the game, if the word almost works and you’re missing one letter, try asking whether you already found it somewhere else.

## 5. Choose your starter word

Now that we know all of that, what actually makes a good starting word?

If **A** is already extremely common, do we really need to start Max with **PALABOK**?

Maybe it makes more sense to test vowels that are harder to find. Maybe an **M** or **P** gives us a useful clue about a possible *unlapi*. Maybe a **U** helps reveal a pattern involving **-UM-**.

Or maybe the best starting word is the same thing we should do to corrupt politicians:

**PUKSAIN.**

Sa eleksyon, *siyempre*.

And yes, maybe **PALABOK** is finally getting replaced.
